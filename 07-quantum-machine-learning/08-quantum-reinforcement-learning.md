# Quantum Reinforcement Learning

## 1. Reinforcement learning is an interaction problem

In reinforcement learning (RL), an agent repeatedly interacts with an environment.

At time $t$:

```text
observe state / observation s_t
-> choose action a_t
-> environment evolves
-> receive reward r_t
-> continue.
```

The goal is not merely to fit labels from a fixed dataset. The agent's actions influence which future data are observed.

Quantum reinforcement learning (QRL) asks how quantum information processing changes the agent, environment, interaction model, or learning complexity.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this chapter, you should be able to:

- define the RL objective and policy,
- distinguish several meanings of “quantum RL,”
- represent a variational quantum policy through measurement probabilities,
- derive a policy-gradient expression conceptually,
- distinguish classical and quantum environments,
- explain why coherent environment access is a strong assumption,
- connect QRL with quantum control and adaptive experiment design,
- and evaluate claimed QRL advantage using interaction/sample complexity rather than model size alone.

## 3. Markov decision process

A classical Markov decision process contains:

- states $s\in\mathcal S$,
- actions $a\in\mathcal A$,
- transition probabilities

```math
P(s'|s,a),
```

- rewards

```math
r(s,a,s'),
```

- discount factor

```math
0\le\gamma<1.
```

A stochastic policy is

```math
\pi_\theta(a|s).
```

## 4. Return

The discounted return from time $t$ is

```math
G_t
=
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\gamma^k r_{t+k}.
```

The learning objective is commonly

```math
J(\theta)
=
\mathbb E_{\pi_\theta}[G_0].
```

The expectation includes randomness from the policy, environment, initial state, and possibly quantum measurements.

## 5. Value functions

The state-value function is

```math
V^\pi(s)
=
\mathbb E_\pi[G_t|s_t=s].
```

The action-value function is

```math
Q^\pi(s,a)
=
\mathbb E_\pi[G_t|s_t=s,a_t=a].
```

Quantum models can be used to parameterize policies, value functions, or both.

## 6. Several meanings of quantum RL

The label QRL can describe very different settings.

### Classical environment, quantum policy

States and rewards are classical, but

```math
\pi_\theta(a|s)
```

is generated by a quantum circuit.

### Classical environment, quantum value model

A quantum model approximates

```math
V(s)
```

or

```math
Q(s,a).
```

### Quantum-enhanced subroutine

A quantum algorithm accelerates a subproblem such as search or estimation under an oracle model.

### Quantum environment

The environment state is a density operator

```math
\rho_t
```

and actions are quantum operations.

### Coherent agent-environment interaction

The interaction remains quantum-coherent rather than being classicalized after every step.

These settings should never be conflated in an advantage claim.

## 7. Variational quantum policy

Let a classical observation $s$ be encoded into

```math
\rho(s).
```

Apply a trainable channel

```math
\mathcal E_\theta.
```

Associate each action with a POVM effect $E_a$.

Then

```math
\pi_\theta(a|s)
=
\mathrm{Tr}
\left[
E_a
\mathcal E_\theta(\rho(s))
\right].
```

The Born rule automatically produces a normalized stochastic policy when

```math
\sum_aE_a=I.
```

## 8. Binary-action example

For two actions, measure a qubit in the computational basis:

```math
\pi_\theta(0|s)
=
\frac{1+\langle Z\rangle_{s,\theta}}{2},
```

```math
\pi_\theta(1|s)
=
\frac{1-\langle Z\rangle_{s,\theta}}{2}.
```

Thus one expectation value determines the complete binary policy.

This is a natural way to convert a variational quantum model into action probabilities.

## 9. Policy-gradient idea

The policy-gradient theorem gives a structure of the form

```math
\nabla_\theta J
=
\mathbb E
\left[
\nabla_\theta
\log\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)
\,Q^{\pi_\theta}(s_t,a_t)
\right].
```

In practice, $Q^{\pi}$ is replaced by Monte Carlo returns, advantages, or a learned critic.

If the policy probabilities come from a PQC, derivatives of

```math
\pi_\theta(a|s)
```

can be estimated through parameter-shift methods under appropriate gate assumptions.

## 10. Two sources of stochasticity

Quantum-policy training can contain both:

```text
environment / trajectory randomness
```

and

```text
finite-shot quantum measurement randomness.
```

Therefore gradient variance can be larger than in ordinary supervised VQC training.

A fair resource estimate should count environment interactions and quantum circuit shots separately.

## 11. Actor–critic architecture

An actor–critic method uses:

- an actor policy

```math
\pi_\theta(a|s),
```

- a critic approximation

```math
V_\phi(s)
```

or $Q_\phi(s,a)$.

Possible hybrid configurations include:

```text
quantum actor + classical critic
classical actor + quantum critic
quantum actor + quantum critic.
```

The phrase “quantum actor–critic” should specify which components are quantum.

## 12. Data re-uploading for policies

Observations can be repeatedly encoded into a policy circuit:

```math
U(s,\theta)
=
\prod_\ell
W_\ell(\theta_\ell)V_\ell(s).
```

This increases classical input expressivity, but it also increases circuit depth and training cost.

The same encoding caveats from supervised QML apply.

## 13. Continuous actions

A quantum circuit naturally outputs bounded probabilities or expectation values.

For continuous control, one can use quantum outputs to parameterize a classical distribution such as

```math
a
\sim
\mathcal N
\left(
\mu_\theta(s),
\sigma_\theta^2(s)
\right),
```

where quantum expectation values determine $\mu$ or other parameters.

Thus continuous-action QRL is usually hybrid rather than a direct measurement-to-action map.

## 14. Quantum environment

A genuinely quantum environment has state

```math
\rho_t.
```

An action can be a control operation or channel

```math
\mathcal A_{a_t}.
```

The environment evolves according to

```math
\rho_{t+1}
=
\mathcal E
\circ
\mathcal A_{a_t}
(\rho_t).
```

The reward can depend on measurement outcomes or state properties.

This connects QRL directly to quantum control.

## 15. Partially observed quantum environments

The agent may not receive a complete classical description of $\rho_t$.

Instead it may obtain:

- a quantum subsystem,
- a weak measurement result,
- a classical observation generated by a POVM,
- a stream of quantum probes.

The problem is then closer to a partially observed dynamical process than an ordinary fully observed MDP.

Quantum memory can become a meaningful agent resource.

## 16. Quantum actions as instruments

An action need not be a unitary gate.

It can be a quantum instrument

```math
\{\mathcal I_a\}_a
```

that simultaneously:

- produces a classical outcome,
- changes the quantum state,
- selects future behavior.

This is natural for adaptive measurement and experiment-design problems.

## 17. Adaptive experiment design

Suppose an unknown physical parameter $\lambda$ controls a quantum system.

At each round, an agent chooses:

```text
probe state
control pulse
interaction time
measurement setting.
```

It receives an observation and chooses the next experiment.

This is naturally formulated as reinforcement learning or Bayesian experimental design.

The objective may be information gain rather than external reward.

## 18. Information-gain reward

A possible reward is reduction in uncertainty about unknown parameter $\Lambda$:

```math
r_t
=
H(\Lambda|D_t)
-
H(\Lambda|D_{t+1}),
```

where $D_t$ is the accumulated data.

Equivalently, the agent can maximize expected mutual information between future measurement outcomes and the unknown parameter.

This makes QRL overlap with quantum metrology and active learning.

## 19. Coherent interaction model

Some theoretical QRL models grant the agent coherent oracle access to the environment transition dynamics.

Instead of receiving one classical transition sample, the agent may access a unitary representation in superposition.

This can enable quantum query speedups.

But the interface is much stronger than ordinary physical trial-and-error interaction.

Therefore any such advantage must state the coherent-access assumption explicitly.

## 20. Classicalized versus coherent agent-environment loop

Two models can be compared:

### Classicalized loop

```text
quantum environment
-> measure
-> classical observation
-> classical/quantum policy
-> classical action command.
```

### Coherent loop

```text
quantum environment
<-> coherent quantum agent
```

with measurement postponed.

The second model can preserve interference and entanglement across interactions.

This is a fundamentally different information-access model.

## 21. Interaction complexity

In RL, the scarce resource is often the number of environment interactions rather than floating-point operations.

Define

```math
N_{\mathrm{env}}
```

as the number of episodes or transitions required to reach a target performance.

A meaningful QRL sample-efficiency claim should compare

```math
N_{\mathrm{env}}^{Q}
```

with

```math
N_{\mathrm{env}}^{C}
```

under comparable interaction interfaces.

## 22. Training cost

If each environment transition triggers a quantum policy evaluation requiring $S$ shots, then raw quantum execution cost scales as

```math
N_{\mathrm{env}}S
```

before gradient overhead.

With $P$ parameters and parameter-shift gradients, a naive policy update can multiply this further.

Thus fewer environment interactions do not automatically mean lower wall-clock training cost.

## 23. Reward-estimation cost

Some quantum environments have rewards that are themselves expectation values:

```math
r
=
\mathrm{Tr}(O\rho).
```

Estimating reward then requires repeated measurements.

The RL loop can therefore contain quantum sampling at both:

```text
policy output
and
reward evaluation.
```

This should be included in resource accounting.

## 24. Exploration

Quantum policies naturally produce stochastic actions through measurement.

But measurement randomness is not automatically useful exploration.

Good exploration requires actions that gather task-relevant information.

A high-entropy quantum policy can still explore inefficiently if its action distribution ignores uncertainty structure.

## 25. Generalization in RL

A policy can overfit to:

- training environments,
- initial-state distributions,
- noise levels,
- specific system sizes.

Useful QRL may require transfer to unseen Hamiltonian parameters, device calibrations, or task instances.

Structured quantum policies can encode physical symmetry or conservation laws as inductive bias.

## 26. Classical baselines

Relevant baselines include:

- policy-gradient methods,
- actor–critic networks,
- model-based RL,
- Bayesian control,
- classical optimal control,
- evolutionary strategies,
- problem-specific quantum-control algorithms.

For a quantum environment, classical agents using optimized measurement strategies can be strong competitors even without a quantum processor.

## 27. What would count as QRL advantage?

Possible advantages include:

### Interaction advantage

Fewer environment uses to reach target performance.

### Query advantage

Fewer calls to a coherently accessible transition oracle.

### Representation advantage

A compact quantum policy represents a useful policy class more efficiently.

### Control advantage

A coherent quantum agent achieves a control objective impossible or expensive under measure-and-classicalize restrictions.

### Communication advantage

A distributed quantum agent exchanges less information.

These should be stated separately.

## 28. Common misconceptions

### “Quantum policy means quantum environment.”

No. A PQC policy can control an entirely classical environment.

### “Measurement randomness automatically provides better exploration.”

No. Exploration quality is task dependent.

### “A coherent quantum-environment oracle is equivalent to ordinary physical interaction.”

No. It is a stronger access model.

### “Higher episodic reward than one classical network proves advantage.”

No. Strong RL and control baselines plus total interaction/runtime accounting are required.

## 29. Worked example: quantum policy for two actions

Suppose state feature $s$ is encoded as

```math
|\phi(s)\rangle
=
R_y(s)|0\rangle.
```

Apply trainable rotation

```math
R_y(\theta).
```

The final state is

```math
R_y(\theta+s)|0\rangle.
```

Measuring in the computational basis gives

```math
\pi_\theta(0|s)
=
\cos^2\left(\frac{\theta+s}{2}\right),
```

```math
\pi_\theta(1|s)
=
\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta+s}{2}\right).
```

This is a stochastic nonlinear policy represented by one qubit.

Its compactness does not by itself imply it is better than a classical logistic/trigonometric policy with similar functional form.

## 30. QRL as experiment-learning architecture

One of the most compelling quantum-native RL directions is not replacing a classical policy network with a PQC.

It is allowing the agent to choose **quantum experiments**:

```text
what state to prepare?
what interaction to apply?
what measurement to perform?
when to stop?
```

The learned object becomes an experimental policy rather than a static classifier.

This connects directly to the question of whether measurement choice itself can be a learnable quantum resource.

## 32. Key takeaways

- Quantum RL is a family of settings, not one architecture.
- A variational quantum policy converts measured quantum probabilities into action distributions.
- RL training contains both trajectory stochasticity and quantum measurement noise.
- Quantum environments naturally connect QRL to control, adaptive measurement, and experiment design.
- Coherent environment access is a strong assumption and must be distinguished from ordinary interaction.
- Interaction complexity, quantum shots, and environment-use cost should be reported separately.
- Quantum-native experiment design may be a deeper QRL direction than replacing a classical policy network with a PQC.
