# Parameterized Quantum Circuits and Ansätze

## 1. Why this distinction matters

Variational quantum algorithms are built from parameterized quantum objects, but several terms are often used interchangeably in the literature even though they describe different conceptual levels.

A **parameterized quantum circuit (PQC)** is a circuit object,

```math
U(\boldsymbol\theta).
```

An **ansatz** is the family of states or transformations generated by some parameterization,

```math
\mathcal A
=
\left\{
U(\boldsymbol\theta)|\psi_0\rangle
:
\boldsymbol\theta\in\Theta
\right\}.
```

A **variational algorithm** additionally specifies a cost estimator and a parameter-update procedure.

Keeping these levels separate prevents a large amount of terminology confusion later in QML.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this chapter, you should be able to:

- define a PQC independently of its task,
- distinguish a circuit realization from the ansatz family it generates,
- write layered PQCs mathematically,
- compare hardware-efficient and problem-inspired ansätze,
- explain expressibility, inductive bias, symmetry, and parameter efficiency,
- identify how architecture choices affect trainability,
- analyze the role of entanglers and circuit depth,
- and explain how data-dependent PQCs become learning models in QML.

## 3. Parameterized quantum circuits

A generic PQC can be written

```math
U(\boldsymbol\theta)
=
U_L(\theta_L)
\cdots
U_2(\theta_2)
U_1(\theta_1).
```

For an initial state $|\psi_0\rangle$,

```math
|\psi(\boldsymbol\theta)\rangle
=
U(\boldsymbol\theta)|\psi_0\rangle.
```

The parameters may be continuous, discrete, shared across gates, or constrained by symmetry.

The term **PQC** says nothing by itself about:

- the objective function,
- whether parameters are optimized,
- whether the circuit is used for chemistry or machine learning,
- whether the output is an expectation value or a quantum state,
- or whether the parameterization is expressive enough for the task.

## 4. Parameterized rotations

A common one-parameter gate is generated by a Hermitian operator $G$:

```math
U(\theta)
=
e^{-i\theta G}.
```

For Pauli-generated rotations,

```math
R_x(\theta)
=
e^{-i\theta X/2},
```

```math
R_y(\theta)
=
e^{-i\theta Y/2},
```

```math
R_z(\theta)
=
e^{-i\theta Z/2}.
```

These gates create smooth parameter dependence and admit analytic gradient rules under appropriate conditions.

## 5. Layered architectures

Many PQCs alternate local rotations and entangling operations:

```math
U(\boldsymbol\theta)
=
\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}
U_{\mathrm{ent}}^{(\ell)}
U_{\mathrm{local}}^{(\ell)}(\boldsymbol\theta_\ell).
```

The local block may contain gates such as

```math
R_z(\theta_{i,\ell}^{(1)})
R_y(\theta_{i,\ell}^{(2)})
R_z(\theta_{i,\ell}^{(3)}),
```

while the entangling block can use CNOT, CZ, native two-qubit gates, or Hamiltonian-generated interactions.

The architecture determines which correlations can be created and how rapidly information spreads across the register.

## 6. What is an ansatz?

An ansatz is a hypothesis about the region of Hilbert space in which a useful solution lies.

For a state problem,

```math
\mathcal A
=
\{|\psi(\boldsymbol\theta)\rangle\}_{\boldsymbol\theta\in\Theta}.
```

For an operator-learning problem, it may instead be a family

```math
\mathcal U
=
\{U(\boldsymbol\theta)\}_{\boldsymbol\theta\in\Theta}.
```

Choosing an ansatz is therefore analogous to choosing a model class in classical machine learning.

A larger ansatz can represent more targets, but this does not imply that it is easier to optimize or statistically preferable.

## 7. Hardware-efficient ansätze

A hardware-efficient ansatz is designed around native device operations and connectivity.

Typical design:

```text
single-qubit rotations
-> native entanglers
-> single-qubit rotations
-> native entanglers
-> ...
```

Potential advantages:

- shallow compiled depth,
- fewer routing operations,
- compatibility with device connectivity,
- simple implementation.

Potential disadvantages:

- weak incorporation of problem structure,
- unnecessary exploration of irrelevant Hilbert-space regions,
- possible symmetry violation,
- and increased risk of concentration or barren plateaus as depth grows.

Hardware efficiency is therefore a hardware property, not a guarantee of algorithmic efficiency.

## 8. Problem-inspired ansätze

A problem-inspired ansatz builds known structure into the circuit.

Examples include:

- particle-number-preserving transformations,
- symmetry-preserving circuits,
- chemistry-inspired coupled-cluster constructions,
- QAOA alternating operators,
- tensor-network-inspired circuit layouts,
- locality-preserving architectures.

The underlying idea is

```math
\text{prior structure}
\rightarrow
\text{smaller relevant hypothesis family}.
```

A restricted family can be advantageous if it excludes states that cannot solve the task.

This is an example of **inductive bias**.

## 9. Symmetry as architectural information

Suppose the target Hamiltonian commutes with a symmetry operator $S$:

```math
[H,S]=0.
```

If the initial state lies in a symmetry sector and every ansatz gate satisfies

```math
[U_j(\theta_j),S]=0,
```

then the entire variational state remains in that sector.

This can reduce the search space and prevent optimization from wasting resources on physically impossible candidates.

Symmetry preservation is often more valuable than raw expressibility.

## 10. Expressibility

Informally, expressibility asks how broad a family of states or transformations the ansatz can represent.

An extremely expressive circuit may approximate a large fraction of Hilbert space. That sounds desirable, but it can introduce two problems:

1. the circuit may behave statistically like a random circuit;
2. gradients of physically relevant costs may become highly concentrated.

Therefore

```math
\text{expressibility}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{trainability}.
```

The optimal ansatz is often not the largest possible family, but the smallest family that contains useful solutions and remains trainable.

## 11. Parameter count is not model capacity by itself

A circuit with $P$ trainable angles does not necessarily have $P$ independent effective degrees of freedom.

Redundancies can arise from:

- commuting gates,
- parameter symmetries,
- periodicity,
- inactive generators,
- gauge-like equivalences,
- and measurement insensitivity.

For example, if a parameterized gate commutes with everything between it and the measured observable, its effect may cancel or become unobservable for that cost.

Thus

```math
\text{number of parameters}
\neq
\text{effective dimension of the model}.
```

## 12. Entanglement structure

Entangling gates allow the ansatz to represent non-product states.

But more entanglement is not automatically better.

Depending on the task, useful architectures may require:

- local entanglement only,
- long-range entanglement,
- low-entanglement tensor-network-like structure,
- or task-specific correlation patterns.

A circuit that rapidly generates generic volume-law entanglement can become difficult to simulate classically, but it can also become difficult to train.

## 13. Depth and light cones

For local circuits, the effect of a parameter can propagate only through its causal light cone.

If a parameter is located far outside the light cone of a local observable, that parameter cannot affect the observable at shallow depth.

This gives a structural relationship between:

```text
circuit connectivity
+ depth
+ observable locality
-> parameter influence.
```

These locality ideas become important in trainability analyses and QCNN-style architectures.

## 14. Data-dependent PQCs

In QML, the circuit often depends on both data $x$ and trainable parameters:

```math
U(x,\boldsymbol\theta).
```

A simple decomposition is

```math
U(x,\boldsymbol\theta)
=
W(\boldsymbol\theta)
U_{\phi}(x),
```

where $U_{\phi}(x)$ is a feature map.

More general models interleave data and trainable gates:

```math
U(x,\boldsymbol\theta)
=
\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}
W_\ell(\boldsymbol\theta_\ell)
U_\ell(x).
```

This is often called data re-uploading.

The same circuit object can therefore be used as:

- a state ansatz in VQE,
- a QAOA layer family,
- a classifier,
- a regressor,
- a policy model,
- or a generative model.

The task is external to the definition of PQC.

## 15. Worked example: a two-qubit ansatz

Consider

```math
U(\theta_1,\theta_2)
=
\mathrm{CNOT}_{1\to2}
\left(
R_y(\theta_1)\otimes R_y(\theta_2)
\right).
```

Starting from $|00\rangle$,

```math
R_y(\theta_1)|0\rangle
=
\cos\frac{\theta_1}{2}|0\rangle
+
\sin\frac{\theta_1}{2}|1\rangle,
```

and similarly for the second qubit.

Before CNOT the state is a product state. After CNOT, generic choices of $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ can produce entanglement.

For example, choosing

```math
\theta_1=\frac\pi2,
\qquad
\theta_2=0
```

gives

```math
\frac{|00\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
```

Thus a very small parameterized architecture already contains product and entangled states in the same ansatz family.

## 16. Ansatz error

Suppose the exact target state is $|\psi_*\rangle$. Even with perfect optimization, the ansatz may not contain it.

A natural representation error is related to

```math
\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}
|\langle\psi_*|\psi(\boldsymbol\theta)\rangle|^2.
```

If the maximum fidelity is far below one, optimization cannot fix the problem.

This motivates separating

```text
representation failure
from
optimization failure.
```

## 17. Architecture design as a resource tradeoff

A useful ansatz should balance:

```text
representation power
trainability
symmetry
circuit depth
noise exposure
measurement cost
hardware connectivity
classical simulability
```

These objectives can conflict.

For example, deeper circuits can increase representation power while simultaneously increasing noise and gradient concentration.

## 18. Common misconceptions

### “PQC and VQA are synonyms.”

No. A PQC is a circuit object; a VQA is an optimization procedure using such an object.

### “Ansatz and PQC mean exactly the same thing.”

Not conceptually. The ansatz is the candidate family; the PQC is one realization of that family.

### “More parameters always improve the model.”

No. Parameters can be redundant and can worsen optimization.

### “More entanglement means more quantum advantage.”

No. Entanglement alone does not establish useful computational separation.

### “Hardware-efficient means computationally efficient.”

No. It means compatible with hardware primitives; algorithmic trainability and advantage remain separate questions.

## 21. Key takeaways

- A PQC is a parameterized circuit object; an ansatz is the family it generates.
- Architecture design determines representation power, symmetry, entanglement structure, and trainability.
- Hardware efficiency and algorithmic efficiency are distinct concepts.
- More parameters or more expressibility are not automatically beneficial.
- Problem structure and inductive bias can be more valuable than generic Hilbert-space coverage.
- The same PQC can serve as a component of VQE, QAOA, or QML depending on the surrounding task and optimization loop.
