# Qubits and Hilbert Spaces

## 1. Hilbert-space language

A finite-dimensional quantum system is represented by a complex Hilbert space $\mathcal H$. For the purposes of quantum computing, this is a complex vector space equipped with an inner product.

A pure state is represented by a unit vector

$$
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal H,
\qquad
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1.
$$

Physical pure states are rays: multiplying a state by a global phase does not change any measurement statistics,

$$
|\psi\rangle\sim e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle.
$$

## 2. Bra-ket notation

A ket is a column vector,

$$
|\psi\rangle=
\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha\\
\beta
\end{pmatrix},
$$

and the corresponding bra is its conjugate transpose,

$$
\langle\psi|=(\alpha^*,\beta^*).
$$

The inner product is

$$
\langle\phi|\psi\rangle,
$$

while the outer product

$$
|\psi\rangle\langle\phi|
$$

is an operator.

## 3. The qubit

A qubit has state space

$$
\mathcal H_2\cong\mathbb C^2.
$$

The computational basis is

$$
|0\rangle=
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
|1\rangle=
\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Every pure qubit state can be written as

$$
|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle,
\quad
|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1.
$$

After removing an irrelevant global phase, an equivalent parameterization is

$$
|\psi\rangle
=
\cos\frac\theta2|0\rangle
+
e^{i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2|1\rangle.
$$

This gives the Bloch-sphere representation developed in the next chapter.

## 4. Operators and observables

Linear operators act on states:

$$
A:\mathcal H\rightarrow\mathcal H.
$$

Important classes include:

- **unitary operators**: $U^\dagger U=I$,
- **Hermitian operators**: $A=A^\dagger$,
- **positive semidefinite operators**: $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle\ge0$,
- **projectors**: $P=P^\dagger=P^2$.

Closed-system quantum gates are represented by unitary operators. Observables are represented by Hermitian operators.

## 5. Pauli operators

The Pauli matrices are

$$
X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Together with the identity they form a basis for linear operators on one qubit. Any Hermitian one-qubit operator can be written

$$
A=a_0I+a_xX+a_yY+a_zZ,
$$

with real coefficients.

The Pauli operators satisfy

$$
XY=iZ,
\qquad
YZ=iX,
\qquad
ZX=iY,
$$

and anticommute pairwise:

$$
\{X,Y\}=\{Y,Z\}=\{Z,X\}=0.
$$

## 6. Unitary time evolution

For a time-independent Hamiltonian $H$, closed-system evolution is

$$
U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}.
$$

In quantum-computing units one often sets $\hbar=1$, giving

$$
U(t)=e^{-iHt}.
$$

Single-qubit rotations are generated by Pauli operators:

$$
R_x(\theta)=e^{-i\theta X/2},
\quad
R_y(\theta)=e^{-i\theta Y/2},
\quad
R_z(\theta)=e^{-i\theta Z/2}.
$$

These gates are central to parameterized quantum circuits.

## 7. Spectral decomposition

Every Hermitian operator admits a decomposition

$$
A=\sum_j a_j |a_j\rangle\langle a_j|,
$$

where the eigenvalues $a_j$ are real and the eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal. This connects linear algebra directly to measurement: projective measurement of $A$ returns an eigenvalue $a_j$ with probability determined by the state overlap with the corresponding eigenspace.

## 8. Key takeaway

The Hilbert-space formalism separates three objects that should not be confused:

$$
\text{state }|\psi\rangle,
\qquad
\text{transformation }U,
\qquad
\text{observable }A.
$$

Most of quantum computing can be built by combining these objects with tensor products and measurement.
